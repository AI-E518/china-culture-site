*A Chinese Traveler Who’s Been Everywhere in China Teaches You How to Play*

> Hi, I’m Lao Ai. The number of times I’ve visited Beijing might sound unbelievable—even the Forbidden City? I’ve taken friends there seven or eight times, and each visit still uncovers something new. Autumn is the season I most want to bring you here: the sky is high and clouds are light; when the ginkgo trees turn golden, their brilliance against the vermilion walls and gilded roofs makes every casual phone snap look stunning. But don’t believe those online posts promising “Three Days, All Major Sights”—that’s rushing, not playing. Trust me: six days, slow and steady, no exhaustion, just deep, rich flavor. This isn’t a generic sightseeing checklist—it’s hard-won wisdom from my own missteps. I’ll tell you exactly how to grab tickets, which gate to enter, and where to eat—no secrets held back.

**First, a few key points—get these straight before you go**

* **Best window**: Mid-October to early November (~40 days). Peak red leaves: late October. Ginkgo at full gold: first week of November.  
* **Ticket booking**: Forbidden City—7 days ahead, 8 p.m.; National Museum—7 days ahead, 5 p.m.; Chairman Mao Memorial Hall—6 days ahead, 12:30 p.m. All via official WeChat accounts.  
* **Three essentials to carry**: Soft-soled shoes (20,000 steps per day is normal); lightweight jacket (10–15°C difference between morning and evening); power bank + ID card.  
* **Two iron rules**: Most museums close on Mondays—plan around it. Use annual park passes or student IDs whenever possible—don’t pay full price blindly.

## Day 1: The Forbidden City’s Central Axis—Master the Main Story First  

I always schedule the Forbidden City for Day 1—not for any other reason than to channel your fresh-off-the-plane excitement right into the city’s most treasured site. Tian’anmen Square is free but requires advance reservation. To climb the Tian’anmen Rostrum (¥15), book 7 days ahead at 5 p.m. The Chairman Mao Memorial Hall opens only in the morning—and reservations open 6 days ahead at 12:30 p.m. All three can easily fit into one morning. Note: There are no restrooms inside the square, and don’t carry too much water—buy it *after* security. Enter via the underground passage to Wu Men (Meridian Gate). Don’t count on buying tickets on-site—that’s gambling, and losing is the norm. If you miss out, you’ll be left with nothing but a photo of the red wall and a long walk home.

The real way to do it: At 8 p.m., exactly 7 days before your visit, log into the official WeChat account “The Palace Museum.” Miss this step, and everything else falls apart. Once inside Wu Men, resist the urge to sprint with your camera. Follow this smooth route: Wu Men → Taihe Dian (Hall of Supreme Harmony) → Zhonghe Dian (Hall of Central Harmony) → Baohe Dian (Hall of Preserving Harmony) → Treasure Gallery (northeast corner—easy detour) → Eastern & Western Six Palaces → Shenwu Men (Gate of Divine Might) exit. The Treasure Gallery and Clock Gallery each cost ¥10—don’t skip them. Gold, jade, and jewels dazzle so fiercely I always linger longer than planned. When exiting Shenwu Men, skip the taxi—Jingshan Park is directly across the street, just a two-minute walk. Save it for tomorrow. One reminder: Water inside the palace is expensive—fill up *before* entering. For guided tours, the audio guide (¥20) is more than enough; group guides shouting through megaphones will leave you with a headache—and less time to truly see.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** The Forbidden City is closed on Mondays—schedule around it. Want photos of empty halls? Enter at opening time (9 a.m.)—by 9:30 a.m., tour groups flood in, filling every frame with backs of heads. With elders or kids, skip the full circuit. Stick to the central axis + Treasure Gallery—the rest can wait for stronger legs.

## Day 2: Jingshan for Panoramas, Then Dive Into Autumn Alleyways  

Day 2 is about easing in—not pushing hard. Start with Jingshan Park, directly opposite the Forbidden City’s north gate. Tickets cost just ¥2. Climb to Wanchun Pavilion, and the entire Forbidden City unfolds beneath you—a sea of golden rooftops. That iconic “Lao Ai Perspective” panoramic shot? This is where it’s taken. For crowd-free shots, arrive *before* 7 a.m.—otherwise, selfie sticks dominate the view. After descending, stroll along Beihai Park. In autumn, cool breezes ripple across the water; willows turn yellow, lotus stems stand bare—another kind of elegant openness. No ticket needed—just walk the lakeside. The real highlight comes in the afternoon: hutongs. Skip Nanluoguxiang’s main street—it’s saturated with tourist commerce. Instead, head north to Beiluoguxiang, Wudao Ying, or Dongsi—where old Beijing still breathes.

I have a habit with friends: explore hutongs *before* eating. Gray-brick courtyards, gnarled trees, autumn underwear hung to dry—this warmth doesn’t exist on main streets. Nearby lies Prince Gong’s Mansion (¥40; book 10 days ahead at 8 p.m.). Follow the route: Yinyin Dian → Xijin Zhai → Houzhao Lou → Fu Chi (Bat Pond). See how skillfully Heshen once hid his treasures. At dusk, settle by Shichahai Lake. As sunset dyes the water orange, Houhai’s rhythm gently rises. Bonus tip: At Yinding Bridge, you can “Yinding Guanshan” (“Silver Ding Viewing Mountains”)—one of the historic “Eight Scenic Spots of Yanjing.” On clear days, you’ll glimpse mountain silhouettes westward—though today’s skyscrapers block much of the view, that unhurried spirit remains.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** Hutong stalls labeled “Old Beijing Snacks”? Half target tourists. For *Luhu* (pork offal stew), go to Xiao Chang Chen in Menkuan Hutong. For *Chaogan* (stewed pork liver), try Tianxingju or Yao Ji. For *Zhajiangmian* (noodles with fried sauce), head to Fangzhuanchang 69 Hao. These are local favorites—skip the main-street spots. Walk just 50 meters farther, and both taste and price improve instantly.

## Day 3: Temple of Heaven’s Ritual Grandeur, Qianmen & Niujie’s Vibrant Flavors  

Day 3 belongs to the Temple of Heaven. First, the biggest pitfall: Standard tickets (¥15) *won’t* get you into the Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests or the Echo Wall. You’ll waste your trip. Buy the combined ticket (¥34) instead—and enter via the East Gate: Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests → Danbi Bridge → Huangqiongyu (the Echo Wall sits in this small courtyard—don’t miss it) → Circular Mound Altar, then exit south. The Temple’s magic isn’t in its size—it’s in its “circle”: ancient cosmology—“Heaven is round, Earth is square,” “Harmony between Heaven and Humanity”—is embedded in every structure. Stand at the center of the Circular Mound and shout—you’ll hear your voice echo back, wrapping around you. Small sites stop selling tickets at 5:30 p.m., and it’s closed Mondays—go early, not late.

In the afternoon, head to Qianmen Dashilar—home to clusters of time-honored brands, all walkable. For dinner, I strongly recommend Niujie (Ox Street)—Beijing’s heartland of halal cuisine: Hongji’s beef buns and sesame cakes, Baiji’s glutinous rice cakes, Jubao Yuan’s hotpot, and Nailuo Wei’s cheese rolls—affordable, authentic, far more genuine than Qianmen’s polished restaurants. A friend once insisted on dining at the Niujie entrance—¥48 for a bowl of zhajiangmian, yet worse than the ¥15 version at the alley’s end. Tell me, is that fair? After dinner, stroll back to your hotel. Today’s steps are lighter—perfect recovery before tomorrow’s garden climb.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** Niujie gets packed at mealtime—arrive before 5:30 p.m. to shorten lines. For *Yangxiezi* (spicy lamb spine hotpot), avoid random spots near tourist zones. Go to trusted local favorites like Jubao Yuan or Mabei’er. Exiting the Temple of Heaven’s West Gate puts you near the Natural History Museum—ideal for families to combine both in one day.

## Day 4: The Imperial Gardens—Yiheyuan & Yuanmingyuan in Autumn  

Day 4 is dedicated to imperial gardens—two adjacent, perfectly sequenced. For the Summer Palace (Yiheyuan), here’s my no-detour route: Enter via the New Palace Gate, take a boat to the Marble Boat, then walk east along the Long Corridor to exit at the East Palace Gate—covering Painting-in-Motion Pavilion, Tower of Buddhist Incense, and more. But I’ll share a personal favorite: If time allows, detour into Houxi River via the North Ruyi Gate. This narrow waterside path, flanked by golden ginkgo reflections and few crowds, is breathtaking—and known mostly to locals. Suzhou Street charges extra and lacks autumn charm—skip it. The park is vast: use boats or electric carts to save your legs. Don’t stubbornly walk it all—water inside is pricey, so bring your own.

From there, hop a few bus stops to Yuanmingyuan (Old Summer Palace). Tickets: ¥10 (basic) or ¥25 (combined). Enter via the East Gate—the closest to the Western-style ruins: Da Shui Fa (Grand Waterworks), Huanghua Zhen (Maze Garden), and the panoramic sand model. With autumn grasses turning gold, the ruins feel especially poignant. Every time I stand before Da Shui Fa, I pause—those five textbook words, “The Burning of Yuanmingyuan,” hit differently when you’re standing *here*. There’s also a 1-kilometer ginkgo avenue in the northwest Qichun Garden section—separate from the ruins. Visit in late September for flower fields, not golden trees. If time is tight, head straight to the Western Ruins; save ginkgos for Day 6 at the Temple of Heaven.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** Both Yiheyuan and Yuanmingyuan are huge—each needs half a day for proper appreciation. Don’t cram them with other sights. I dedicate this whole day to just these two, then rest early—so I’m ready to tackle the Great Wall on Day 5.

## Day 5: The Great Wall & Bird’s Nest/Water Cube Night Lights  

Day 5 means leaving the city—no Beijing trip is complete without the Great Wall. Badaling is famous and well-equipped: Take the cable car up to the North 7th Tower, then hike to the North 8th Tower (the “Hero’s Slope”)—effortless and scenic. But honestly? I prefer Mutianyu—fewer crowds, richer autumn colors, and photogenic views. Its cable car + toboggan ride delights kids too—though its lower profile means you’ll need to research it yourself. Avoid “one-day tours” with signs at Qianmen—they often divert you to Shuiguan or wild sections, plus shopping stops. Take the official S2 suburban railway (departing Beijing North Station, just a few yuan) or Bus 877 from Deshengmen—cheaper and reliable.

The Wall is windy—summit temperatures run several degrees colder than downtown—so bring that jacket. With elders or kids, skip the full climb. Cable car up and down to the Hero’s Slope is enough for a meaningful stamp. If your legs ache, keep tonight light—no packed schedules. Don’t crash immediately upon returning—head to the Bird’s Nest and Water Cube for night views. Lit up in shimmering blue, they’re a world apart from daytime’s ancient architecture—the face of modern Beijing shines brightest here. My plan: Exhausted from the Wall? Enjoy a relaxed night view and a good meal—no forced walking. Otherwise, Day 6 won’t start well.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** The Wall is 1.5–2 hours from downtown—leave before 8 a.m. for stability. Don’t pair it with distant red-leaf routes on the same day—traffic eats all your time. During evening rush hour, avoid surface transport—switching subway lines beats taxis.

## Day 6: National Museum Finale, Ginkgo Avenue Grand Finale  

The final day belongs to the National Museum—free but fiercely competitive. Book at 5 p.m., exactly 7 days ahead—hesitate, and it’s gone. This is my chosen grand finale: The Forbidden City shows *how emperors lived*; the National Museum reveals *where the Chinese people came from*. Enter via the North Gate for speed, then head straight to the B1 Ancient China Exhibition—Houmuwu Ding (the largest ancient bronze vessel), Four Rams Ritual Vessel, terracotta figures—five millennia in one sweeping journey. I always spend half a day here. Bring your ID—no lighters past security. With kids? Swap the National Museum for the Natural History Museum (book 3 days ahead at 11 a.m.). Its B1 Dinosaur Park keeps children engaged for hours—and it’s near the Temple of Heaven, making a combo easy.

Don’t waste the afternoon—ginkgo time. The most famous ginkgo avenue runs outside the Diaoyutai State Guesthouse—but it’s packed. For serene shots, choose Ditan Park (enter via West Gate—golden ginkgo against red walls; *my personal pick for Beijing’s quintessential autumn portrait*) or Zhongshan Park, or the lesser-known Sanli River (near Qianmen, riverside, quiet). Don’t delay ginkgo photos until mid-November—wind strips leaves fast. The first week of November is the sweet spot—miss it, and you’ll wait till next year. Snap your final shots, wrap up your six days, and catch your flight—or extend your stay.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** The National Museum closes Mondays—don’t schedule it then. Galleries are large and air-conditioned—bring a light long-sleeve. Food inside is overpriced and underwhelming—eat outside. You can also squeeze in Yonghegong (Lama Temple): Its autumn ginkgo-lined paths are sublime (¥25; enter via Zhaotai Gate, proceed to Wanhua Pavilion—serene, warm, deeply reverent).

## Pre-Trip Essentials—Get These Sorted First  

Now that we’ve covered six days, let’s consolidate cross-site practicalities—lessons learned from my own stumbles or friends’ blunders.  

1. **Reservations are non-negotiable**: Forbidden City, National Museum, Chairman Mao Memorial Hall, and Natural History Museum—all booked via their official WeChat accounts. Set alarms. Don’t wait until arrival.  
2. **Transportation**: The subway is your best friend. Download the “Beijing Subway” app or use Alipay’s transit QR code—no physical card needed. For buses, scan *both* on boarding *and* alighting—forgetting the latter incurs extra charges. Avoid surface transport during rush hours (7:30–9 a.m., 5:30–7 p.m.)—Lines 1, 2, and 4 get crushingly crowded.  
3. **Payments**: Alipay and WeChat now support foreign cards—QR codes work almost everywhere. Still, carry ¥100–200 cash for street vendors and public toilets.  
4. **Clothing**: Autumn brings big temperature swings—20°C+ in daylight, dropping below 10°C after sunset. Layer like an onion. Soft-soled shoes matter more than anything.  
5. **Restrooms**: Lines form at major attractions—use facilities *before* entering. Public toilets in hutongs are often cleaner than those inside parks.  
6. **Pitfalls to avoid**: Locals skip Wangfujing’s snack street; bargain aggressively at Xiu Shui Market; many “time-honored” Qianmen brands are imitations—stick to the eateries I listed earlier.  
7. **Saving money**: Student IDs grant half-price entry; Beijing’s annual park pass pays off if you’re staying long. Mondays and Tuesdays are least crowded—many museums close Mondays anyway.  
8. **Essential apps**: Gaode Maps (navigation), Dazhong Dianping (food discovery + authentic reviews), Hanglv Zongheng (flights), 12306 (trains).  
9. **Xiangshan’s red leaves?** Famous—but peak season brings overwhelming crowds. Weekday afternoons mean seeing heads, not leaves. Skip unless you’re obsessed. Ditan, Zhongshan, and Dajuesi/Po Feng Ling offer equally stunning ginkgo and red leaves—with breathing room.  
10. **Autumn days shorten fast**: Darkness falls by 5 p.m. Don’t overpack your itinerary—leave evenings open for unhurried hutong strolls at golden hour.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** Beijing’s golden autumn lasts just ~40 days—peak red leaves in late October, full ginkgo gold in early November. Hit this window, and what you see matches the photos online. Come too early? Everything’s green. Too late? Bare branches. It’s a one-shot chance—don’t miss the timing.

## Where to Stay & Where to Eat  

I’ve thought this through for you. First-timers prioritizing convenience should stay near Qianmen Street, Chongwenmen, Zhushikou, Ping’anli, or Caishikou—subway hubs with dual-line access, within walking distance of Tian’anmen and the Forbidden City, eliminating early-morning flag-raising marathons. For authentic hutong charm, choose courtyard hotels around Shichahai—but *always* confirm “Can cars reach the door?” before booking. Many inner-ring hutongs forbid vehicles—dragging luggage over stone slabs is brutal, especially with elders or kids. Wangfujing and Guomao host upscale hotels—if budget allows. Beijing South Railway Station suits high-speed rail travelers—close to the Temple of Heaven and Taoranting Park.

For food, follow this map:  
• *Snack districts*: Qianmen Dashilar, Niujie, Guijie (Ghost Street), and Huguo Temple.  
• *Hotpot*: Jubao Yuan, Beimen Shuanrou, Jingmen Lao Baosan.  
• *Authentic Beijing cuisine*: Shijiu, Tongchunyuan, Sishi Tongtang.  
• *Hutong-style dining*: Look for hidden gems—“seven searches, eight finds.”  
• *Snacks*: Hongji, Baiji, Nailuo Wei, Sanyuan Mei Yuan, Hongxing Qianjin Milk & Bakery.  
• *Late-night cravings*: Hu Da for spicy crayfish.  

One rule: Never sit down at the first restaurant you see near a tourist site. Walk 50 meters—authenticity and value return instantly. My habit? Arrive somewhere, open Dazhong Dianping, search “locals frequent,” sort by *distance* (not “recommended”—those are often ads).

> Done—six days mapped out. Beijing isn’t meant to be “checked off”—it’s meant to be *wandered*. Slow down, and the red walls will finally whisper their stories to you. When you actually come, follow this route—I guarantee it’s smoother than any generic checklist. Next time you want to explore another city, just say the word. I’ll take you—from one plane ticket—to discover a China that’s vast, yet profoundly gentle.
