When writing about Beijing, I organized the itinerary by day—six days, one continuous route. For Shanghai, I don’t organize by day—I organize by *river*.

Almost everyone visiting Shanghai for the first time stands on this side of the Huangpu River, faces Lujiazui, and takes *one* photo—then assumes they’ve “seen” Shanghai. But Shanghai was actually raised by *two* rivers: one is the **Huangpu River**, responsible for postcard views; the other is the **Suzhou Creek**, responsible for daily life. The former is wide, bright, and staged for visitors; the latter is narrow, weathered, lined with old warehouses, flour mills, textile factories, and apartment buildings’ rear façades—where locals walk their dogs, jog, or carry groceries home.

In my guide *Lao Ai Takes You Around Shanghai*, I mentioned the ¥2 ferry—it’s a commuter service, part of local daily life. This piece is about something else: how to follow this narrow creek into a Shanghai that doesn’t perform for you.

## A Half-Day Walk: From the Bund Origin Westward

Begin at the **Bund Origin** (around South Suzhou Road). First, visit the **Shanghai Postal Museum**—free, no reservation required. The building itself is a protected cultural relic; just step inside and look up at the grand hall—it’s worth more than many paid attractions. Exit and head north across **Zhapu Road Bridge**. Privately, I consider this vantage point the *best* in all of Shanghai: behind you looms the steel truss of Waibaidu Bridge; ahead, you can frame Lujiazui perfectly—and yet it’s far less crowded than the Bund observation deck.

Continue west to the **Sihang Warehouse**. Don’t rush to photograph the bullet-riddled wall—just stand there quietly for a moment. Locals don’t visit often, but when they do, they’re always hushed.

Follow the riverside promenade further west, passing Changhua Road and Mengqingyuan. There are no “attractions” here—just anglers, aunties doing group calisthenics, and uncles walking very old dogs. Arrive at **M50 (No. 50 Moganshan Road)**, a former textile mill transformed into a contemporary art district, with galleries lining the street—most open free of charge.

There’s a Shanghai dialect term especially fitting for this day: **“dàng mǎ lù”** (*to wander the streets with no destination*). It’s similar to the French *flâneur*, but Shanghainese aren’t quite so literary—they simply stroll after dinner, not to buy anything, not to take photos—just to move.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: **Don’t go to M50 on Mondays**. Many galleries close that day; though the compound remains open, there’s little to see. When the梧桐 (plane-tree-lined) areas get packed on weekend afternoons, try walking *back* toward the riverside instead—the breeze is stronger, crowds thinner, and the atmosphere more relaxed.

## Two Very Different Boats—If You Want to Ride One

Let’s clarify upfront—don’t mix them up:

The **¥2 ferry** is a *transportation service*, not a sightseeing cruise. Shanghai Municipal Bureau of Culture and Tourism lists it plainly on its English website: 17 routes citywide, nearly 34 piers—**¥2 for pedestrians, ¥2.8 for bicycles and e-bikes, ¥4 for motorcycles**, payable via Alipay transit code or Shanghai Public Transport Card. It takes ~10 minutes to cross; the second-deck cabin has air conditioning. So cheap, it hardly feels like a tourist attraction.

Three routes offer the best views—but each has its own personality:  
- The **Dongjin Line** (Jinling East Road ↔ Dongchang Road) faces Lujiazui’s skyline head-on—the most direct angle—but runs **daytime only**, ending around 6:15 p.m., so no night views.  
- The **Dongfu Line** (Fuxing East Road ↔ Dongchang Road) operates until *after 9 p.m.*—choose this for night scenery.  
- The **Taigong Line** (Gongping Road ↔ Taitongzhan) is the least crowded, nicknamed by locals the “Most Beautiful Sunset Line”—departs at 6 p.m. Schedules and last departures change regularly; check the official **“Shanghai Ferry” WeChat account** for real-time updates before heading out.

The **Suzhou Creek Cruise**, however, is entirely different. Jiu Shi Suzhou Creek Cruises now operate from multiple docks—including Bund Origin, Sihang Warehouse, Changhua Road, Changfeng Park, Mengqingyuan, and Zhongshan Park. Rides range from 15 minutes to 90 minutes. Publicly listed fares I found: ¥30 for the 15-minute trip from Bund Origin to Sihang Warehouse; ¥60 for a 30-minute loop; ¥160 for the full 90-minute journey from Bund Origin to Changfeng Park. Schedules vary by dock and season—**always check the day’s timetable before departure**; don’t rely on these figures alone.

This boat glides along the industrial *backside* of the city: bridges hang low overhead, banks are lined with repurposed warehouses and converted residences—and as the vessel moves slowly, you begin to see how this city was built, layer upon layered. It’s a completely different temperament from the Huangpu River cruises’ “light-and-laser spectacle.”

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: Pick the **evening cruise**, departing just before sunset—lights come on as dusk falls, letting you experience *both* sides of Shanghai in one ride. Ask crew members onboard, *“Which side faces south?”* before settling in—choose your seat accordingly. The creek bends constantly; sit on the wrong side, and you’ll spend the whole trip staring at brick walls.

## Shanghai Turned Factories Into Art Museums

This is what I most want to tell foreign friends.

What *was* **Xuhui Riverside** (the West Bank)? An airport runway, oil tanks, a cement factory. Today, along its five-kilometer waterfront stand Long Museum, West Bund Museum, and Oil Tank Art Center. Runners pass right by entrances to oil-painting exhibitions; dogs nap beside decommissioned aircraft hangars.

At the other end lies **Yangpu Riverside**. Walking from Yangshupu Waterworks toward Lvzhi Qiu (“Green Hill”), you’ll see original steel frames, conveyor belts, and mooring bollards preserved exactly as they were—not freshly painted or polished. Across the river? Lujiazui. This area’s redevelopment feels more restrained—and more enduring—than the West Bank’s.

When a city chooses to retain its oldest, most unglamorous industrial bones and transform them into public space—rather than bulldozing them for shopping malls—that decision speaks louder about Shanghai’s character than any new landmark ever could.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: The riverside offers almost no shade, and wind gusts run two levels stronger than downtown. In November, bring a windproof jacket—don’t dress for the city-center temperature. That riverside chill seeps straight into your bones.

## A Few Blunt Truths

Skip the **Bund observation deck** on holidays—not because it’s unimpressive, but because all you’ll see is the back of strangers’ heads. For night views, try Zhapu Road Bridge, the Lujiazui circular skywalk, or simply hop on that ¥2 ferry—far better than squeezing against the railing.

Locals *almost never* ride the **Bund Sightseeing Tunnel**. I won’t call it bad—I’ll just say I’ve lived here over twenty years and have *never* taken a single friend there voluntarily.

The **sightseeing bus** (¥80–¥100 for a one-day pass) isn’t forbidden—but Shanghai’s most compelling sights lie at ground level: in *lilong* alleyways, along riverside paths, under plane trees. The bus drops you off then pulls away. Two legs + one ¥2 ferry delivers vastly better value.

To truly understand Shanghai, stop photographing the opposite bank from the shore. Turn around—and walk toward that narrow river.
