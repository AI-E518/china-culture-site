If you ask a Beijinger, “What do you eat in winter?” eight out of ten won’t say *Peking duck*—they’ll say: *yang xie zi* (lamb spine hotpot). Peking duck is for show—impressing guests. *Yang xie zi* is what insiders share late at night: a steaming, soul-warming pot.

## What Is It?

*Yang xie zi* refers to lamb spinal bones—the vertebrae with marrow and clinging meat. Simmered slowly in clear or red broth, served with napa cabbage, wide rice noodles, and frozen tofu, it’s all about gnawing the bones and sucking out the rich marrow. Originating as a northern Chinese halal meat preparation, it evolved into Beijing’s most ubiquitous autumn–winter restaurant dish—hotpot shops serving *yang xie zi* line nearly every street.

## My Take

I truly fell in love with *yang xie zi* during a business trip to Beijing. I met up with an old colleague—a longtime industry veteran—and he took me to a *yang xie zi* hotpot spot on Guijie (Ghost Street). The pot that arrived was piled high with lamb spinal bones—each piece slightly shorter than a forearm; a full spine typically cut into just three or four segments, still holding marrow and flecks of meat. That pure, unadulterated lamb flavor—paired with the unmistakable craft and *guo qi* (wok energy) of authentic Beijing hotpot—won me over instantly.

Ever since, no trip to Beijing feels complete without seeking out a good *yang xie zi* hotpot and savoring it thoroughly. Unlike Peking duck, it needs no fanfare—just one steaming pot, hands-on eating, and marrow-sucking gusto. You’ve got to *“love this particular thing”* to truly get it.

## Why Foreigners Are Hooked

*Yang xie zi* ranks among the most authentically Chinese ways to eat—hands-on gnawing, marrow-sucking, gathering around the pot. It’s kin to Western ribs or French bone marrow—but far more everyday, far more neighborhood-rooted. And crucially, it’s *almost impossible to find abroad*: you won’t find a single overseas Chinese restaurant serving authentic *yang xie zi* hotpot. This “disappears beyond China’s borders” quality makes it precisely the kind of local, unexported taste that sparks foreign curiosity about “real China.” A word of caution: it’s bold, gamey, and pungent—addictive only if you *“love this particular thing.”* And that, ironically, is its best story.

## Where to Go · How to Start

In Beijing’s autumn or winter, simply walk into any *yang xie zi* hotpot restaurant—whether a time-honored name like *Man Peng Xuan* or *Xie Zi Li*, or a bustling local favorite on any corner—and order a pot, plus some baked flatbreads (*shao bing*). Skip the formality—go for the most down-to-earth spot you can find. On your first visit, start by sucking the marrow—it’s the soul of the whole pot.
