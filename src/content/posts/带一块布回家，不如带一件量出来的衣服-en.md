An Italian friend spent four days in Shanghai. On the morning of Day Three, he suddenly asked me: “Can I get a suit made here?”

I said yes—but you only have two days left. Yet he pulled it off: measurements on Day One, first fitting on Day Three, pickup before departure—and he boarded his flight wearing his brand-new jacket. I’ve mentioned this story several times—not because the suit was extraordinary, but because it reveals a side of Shanghai that’s easily overlooked: **for a century, this city has been tailoring clothes for people—and doing it differently from anywhere else.**

Most tourists’ shopping lists include fridge magnets, silk scarves, and tea. Those are fine. But if you’re willing to set aside half a day, Shanghai offers something far more personal.

![](/media/uploads/caifeng04.jpg)

## First, something official—just announced

At the municipal government’s press conference for “Shanghai Summer 2026,” the Shanghai Municipal Commission of Commerce specifically highlighted its new “Shanghai Customization” service for inbound visitors—and named three designated clusters: **the South Bund Tailoring Center, the Yuyuan Gold & Jewelry Customization Zone, and the International Eyewear City**. The international version of airline-travel app Hanglv Zongheng has launched a dedicated “Shanghai Customization” section, offering multilingual guidance for these three locations. Gaode Maps’ English edition has also rolled out a “Shanghai Customization” consumer map, linking together clothing tailoring, jewelry customization, and optometry/eyewear fitting.

I was genuinely pleased when I first saw this list—not because we now have another map, but because it finally translates something locals take for granted (“Shanghai *does* custom work”) into something foreign visitors can actually find. Before, if you wanted a garment made, you needed a local friend to guide you.

## Two National Intangible Cultural Heritage Sites on Shaanxi North Road

Head toward Jing’an District, and at No. 199–201 Shaanxi North Road stands **Hengsheng**, while next door at No. 207–209 is **Longfeng Qipao**. Both were inscribed onto the **National List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2011**: Hengsheng for its “Hengsheng Fengbang Tailoring Techniques,” and Longfeng for its “Longfeng Qipao Handmade Craftsmanship.” In Jing’an District’s seventh batch of district-level intangible cultural heritage bearers—announced in November 2025—both items remain listed, each with one active inheritor still practicing.

![](/media/uploads/caifeng03.jpg)

“Fengbang” refers to tailors from Fenghua, Ningbo. In the 1920s, they arrived in Shanghai to open Western-style suit shops—mastering Western cutting techniques, then adapting them to Chinese body shapes. Founded in 1929, Hengsheng emphasizes the “**Four Skills**”: cutting, sewing, hand-stitching, and pressing—followed by the “Nine Postures” of shaping. Simply put: a great suit isn’t just about the cut—it’s about how the iron “pushes” fabric to sculpt the shoulders and chest.

Longfeng speaks another language entirely: inlaying, embedding, edging, binding,镂空 (openwork), carving, embroidery, coiling, and painting—nine meticulous hand processes. The Shanghai-style qipao differs most from traditional versions by borrowing Western elements like waist-nipping, darts, and set-in sleeves—making the garment follow the body, rather than swaddle it. Even the knotted buttons are handmade; crafting a single magnolia blossom can take half a day.

Comparing it to London’s Savile Row gets you halfway there. The other half is different: since the city’s opening as a treaty port, Shanghai has been a place where Chinese and Western craftsmanship continually adapt *to each other*—not a pure extension of British tradition. Pricing, turnaround time, and fabric selection are also notably more flexible. If you just want off-the-rack clothing, skip this altogether—what’s sold here is *time*, measured in skilled labor hours.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: Bring a **photo** of the garment you wear best—not verbal descriptions. Shoulder line, sleeve length, waist taper point: a master tailor grasps it instantly. I’ve watched friends gesture for ten minutes, only to resolve everything with one photo pulled up on their phone.

## How to fit customization into a four-day itinerary

The biggest risk in custom tailoring isn’t cost—it’s time. Here’s how I typically schedule it:

* **Day 1**: Choose your tailor, browse fabrics, get measured. Don’t rush to confirm—feel at least three different materials first.
* **Day 3**: Try on the semi-finished piece (masters call this “sample fitting”). This is your last chance to adjust shoulders, waist, and length—after this, major changes become difficult.
* **Day 4 or 5**: Pick up your finished item.

  ![](/media/uploads/qipao03.jpg)

With only two days, skip suits or qipao. Opt instead for shirts, eyewear, or even ready-to-wear. Rushed pieces will disappoint once you wear them back home.

**Eyewear** is the most underestimated option along this route. At the International Eyewear City, optometry and lens fitting move much faster—many customers walk away with glasses the same day or the next. Lens options are broader than most expect. I’ve accompanied friends through several fittings—the final price depends on lens type and coating, so avoid comparing others’ quotes directly to your own. Overall, however, costs remain significantly more accessible than in Europe. **Gold & jewelry customization** in the Yuyuan area is priced per gram plus processing fees—so ask for the full breakdown *before* placing your order. Gold prices are transparent; labor fees are the variable.

A few other places worth a detour: **Peiluomeng**, founded in 1928 (with branches including No. 249 Huashan Road); **Hanyi**, at No. 271 Anhua Road in Changning District (by appointment only); and **Guo Pei Shanghai Haute Couture Salon**, at No. 468 Wai Ma Road in Huangpu District (requires phone reservation at least one day in advance). The latter two do *not* accept walk-ins—don’t make the trip for nothing.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: Ask upfront: “**Can you ship internationally?**” Many shops offer express international shipping—your finished suit arrives at home, saving precious suitcase space and sparing you the worry of a crumpled jacket buried at the bottom of your luggage. Shipping often costs less than buying an extra checked bag.

## And don’t miss Zhangyuan—right along the way

This route has an added bonus: walking from Nanjing West Road to Shaanxi North Road takes you straight through **Zhangyuan**.

Built in 1882, old Shanghai hailed it as “the premier garden of the Shanghai coast.” What remains today is a cluster of shikumen residences—Chinese-style roofs atop Western-style row houses, a deliberate fusion that gave rise to Shanghai’s uniquely hybrid residential aesthetic. Zhangyuan’s East Zone opens on June 30, 2026, with a nearly 100-meter-long landscaped promenade connecting Xingye Taikoo Hui, China Resources Centre, and Zhangyuan itself. The West Zone has been open for over three years, hosting more than fifty “first-in-Shanghai” flagship stores. Entry is free—no tickets required.

I love guiding people through here because it completes the story behind “customization”: such neighborhoods—and the people who live in them—gave rise to such tailors in the first place. Spend the morning browsing fabrics, then enjoy coffee in a shikumen alleyway in the afternoon—your day flows naturally, far more comfortably than rushing between three tourist sites.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: Don’t haggle too hard on labor fees. These shops charge for *craftsmanship*, not markup. Aggressive bargaining usually means sacrificing a step in the process—not reducing the shop’s profit margin. To save meaningfully, choose simpler fabrics or fewer details—not fewer hands-on steps.

At the end of the day, fridge magnets go into drawers. A garment made precisely for your body? You’ll wear it for years—and every time you do, you’ll remember that street, and the tailor holding the soft tape measure.
