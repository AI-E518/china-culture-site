Taking foreign friends around Shanghai, I have a slightly unscrupulous habit: dragging them out the door at 6 a.m. on Day One.

Last year, a friend from Berlin stood at the entrance of a *longtang* (Shanghai alleyway), glanced around, and asked me: “Restaurants aren’t open yet, right?”

I replied, “This breakfast is almost wrapping up.”

The most un-touristy meal in Shanghai happens within this single hour. If you step out at 9 a.m., you’ll see an entirely different city—cafés are just rolling up their shutters; the plane-tree-lined neighborhoods slowly stir awake; young people line up holding paper cups. Both cities are real—but they don’t know each other. In my guide *Lao Ai Takes You Around Shanghai*, I wrote about a local’s typical day—but for breakfast, I gave only one fleeting shot. This article expands that moment fully.

## 6:00–7:00 a.m.: The Four Heavenly Kings  

![](/media/uploads/four-heavenly-kings_-it’s-just-breakfast_1_miss桃心🍑-friends_来自小红书网页版.jpg)

Da bing (sesame flatbread), youtiao (deep-fried crullers), cifi fan (glutinous rice rolls), and doujiang (soy milk)—Shanghainese call these four staples the **“Four Heavenly Kings”** (*sì dà jīn gāng*—literally “four heavenly guardian deities”). Yes, they name their breakfast after celestial protectors. That tells you how seriously they take it.

A full set costs around ¥10. You can eat standing up, walking, or even squatting on a doorstep. Don’t look for seats—look for lines.

Two items will make foreigners pause on first sight:

**Cifi fan** is glutinous rice wrapped around a youtiao, shaped into a compact ball—available in both savory and sweet versions. The sweet version is sprinkled with white sugar and sesame; the savory one stuffed with shredded pickled mustard tuber and pork floss. Locals themselves debate for half an hour over whether the savory or sweet version is “authentic.” Don’t pick sides—just order one of each.

**Xian jiang** (“salty soy milk”) is even more intriguing. It’s not sweet. Instead, dried shrimp, purple laver (nori), minced pickled mustard tuber, and crumbled youtiao are placed at the bottom of the bowl—and then piping-hot soy milk is poured over them, causing a delicate, tofu-like curd to form on top. When I first took a friend to try it, he stared into his bowl for a long time, then said, “This is the umami bomb of breakfast.” I thought that description was sharper than anything I could’ve come up with.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: Menus at such small eateries are often handwritten on walls—no English, sometimes not even photos. The easiest way? Point and say, *“Zhè ge, yí ge”* (“This one, one portion”). Avoid saying *“Ā lā yào gē ge”* (“We want this!”)—it’s *too* local and may leave your server momentarily baffled. Add *“Là yóu yào fá?”* (“Want chili oil?”) and shake your head if you don’t. Don’t worry—the aunties behind the counter handle ten nonverbal foreigners every day.

## 7:30 a.m.: One Shengjian, and a Debate Begins  

Shanghainese don’t call them “pan-fried buns”—they call them **“shengjian mantou”**. In Jiangnan (the lower Yangtze region), the naming system for wheat-based foods differs from northern China’s: in the north, any filled steamed bun is a *baozi*, but in Jiangnan, *all* steamed wheat products—including meat-filled, vegetable-filled, or red-bean-paste-filled ones—are called *mantou*. Even knees are jokingly called *jiǎo mantou* (“foot mantou”). This isn’t linguistic pedantry—it’s the key to understanding orders.

![](/media/uploads/南翔小笼包_1_xxqqj_来自小红书网页版.jpg)

There are two schools of shengjian—and locals *genuinely* argue about them:

**The “Clear Broth” School**, represented by **Da Hu Chun**, founded in 1932. Made with fully leavened dough, its skin is thick, soft, and chewy. The filling contains no aspic (gelatinous stock), and the pleats face upward. No broth bursts forth when you bite in—instead, you taste the natural juices of the meat itself. Old-school Shanghainese savor its wheat fragrance and comforting heft; my father’s generation swears by it.

**The “Cloudy Broth” School** uses semi-leavened dough plus aspic, with pleats facing downward so they stick to the pan while frying. A single bite releases rich broth. **Dong Tai Xiang** is the most technically refined practitioner of this style—its semi-fermented method is listed as a municipal-level intangible cultural heritage. **Xiao Yang Shengjian**, founded in 1994, pushes further: ultra-thin skins, large size, and explosive broth. Traditionalists dismiss it as “inauthentic,” but young locals and tourists are instantly won over.

And here’s something new: **Luo Chun Ge**, widely acknowledged as the original “ancestor” of shengjian, moved its street stall into a teahouse back in 1930—then vanished for twenty-one years. It officially relaunched in 2026. As I write this, queues stretch down the block—minimum 40 minutes. You’re welcome to join the buzz—but don’t schedule it before catching a flight.

Pricing, roughly: ¥10–¥13.5 for a *liǎng* (a unit of weight equal to four pieces), plus a bowl of curry beef vermicelli soup and a few extras—under ¥20 total. Heritage brands adjust prices frequently, so check the price board outside the shop before you go. Don’t rely on my numbers—or on any guide lacking a clear publication date.

There’s a rhyme for eating shengjian:  
**“Gently lift, slowly move, first open the window, then sip the soup.”**  
In plain terms: Take a small bite first, suck out the broth, and *don’t* pop the whole thing into your mouth. I’ve seen too many people scald their tongues on the first bite—then stubbornly insist, “It’s delicious!”

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: To judge whether a breakfast stall is worth queuing for, ignore queue length—watch the *demographics*. If it’s all travelers dragging suitcases and filming the wok with their phones, that line is for tourists. Also: shengjian must be eaten *on-site*. Delivery or takeaway ruins it—after just ten minutes, the crisp bottom layer turns soggy. That’s like eating it for nothing.

## After 9:00 a.m.: Noodles, Guotie, and Another City  

Shengjian is traditionally paired with curry beef vermicelli soup—locally known as *“Yí kè shēngjiān, wǎn tāng, luò wèi”* (“One serving of shengjian, one bowl of soup—settles comfortably in the stomach”). I love this phrase. It’s more precise than *“satisfying”*: it means gentle, unflashy, perfectly balanced comfort.

![](/media/uploads/_1_乐此不疲养生之_来自小红书网页版.jpg)

Later in the morning comes noodles and *guotie* (pan-fried dumplings). **Si Ru Chun**, on Shiquan Road, is a nationally recognized time-honored brand. In cold weather, its clear-broth guotie skip green onions and sesame—relying purely on masterful heat control for a golden, fragrant crust. At **Zhen Ru Lamb Restaurant**, on Siqian Street, the braised lamb noodle recipe is inscribed on Shanghai’s intangible cultural heritage list—a formula passed down since the Qianlong era (1736–1795), yielding tender, intact lamb chunks. Neither spot lies in tourist zones; both are named in official municipal food guides. I’ve taken friends there—and sat down to a room full of authentic local accents.

Around 10 a.m., the plane-tree-lined cafés finally begin opening their doors. Shanghai has lived two rhythms: the steam rising from *longtang* kitchens at 6 a.m., and the pour-over coffee under the plane trees at 10 a.m. They’re separated by just four streets—and by a century.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: On weekends, expect queues at heritage brands after 10 a.m. Want to try everything? Split breakfast across two days: one morning at 6 a.m. for the Four Heavenly Kings, another at 8:30 a.m. for shengjian + soup. Don’t cram all four into one morning—I’ve tried it. Lunch that day is ruined.

## A Few Blunt Truths  

I don’t recommend the long queues for “crab roe xiaolongbao” beside the Nine-Turn Bridge at Yuyuan Garden. Not because they’re bad—just because those lines are for *seats*, not flavor. For real crab roe, visit **Wang Jia Sha** or **Guang Ming Cun** between September and November. Crab roe shengjian is seasonal—once winter ends, it’s gone. These shops won’t substitute frozen fillings.

Also, don’t treat Shanghai breakfast as a “check-in item.” Parisians don’t photograph their croissants before posting them online; Shanghainese don’t snap selfies with soy milk and youtiao while rushing for the metro. Do *that*, and you’ll get closer to this city than any hundred photos ever could.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: Salty soy milk, cifi fan, and clear-broth shengjian may not win you over on first bite. That’s normal. Roughly one-third of my friends frowned on their first try—then returned the next day to buy more. Don’t rush to judgment. Give them two chances.
