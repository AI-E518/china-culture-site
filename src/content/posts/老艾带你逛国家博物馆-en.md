> Hi, I’m Lao Ai. I’ve been to Beijing more than ten times—and visited the National Museum of China (NMC) several times too. My first visit? **First, here are some key facts—keep them in mind**

* **Full name**: National Museum of China (located just east of Tian’anmen Square)  
* **Admission**: Free—but advance reservation is mandatory  
* **Scale**: Plan for *at least* half a day; it’s far larger than you imagine  
* **Core highlight**: The “Ancient China” permanent exhibition on B1 (basement level)—home to most of the museum’s iconic masterpieces  
* **Must-see artifacts**: Houmuwu Ding (the largest ancient bronze vessel), Four-Goat Square Zun, Jade Burial Suit with Gold Threads, Tang Sancai (Tang tri-color glazed pottery), and the Nine-Dragon Nine-Phoenix Crown  
* **One crucial tip**: Wear comfortable shoes—this is *not* a joke

## First things first: The NMC isn’t just *any* museum  

Let’s get this straight before you even step inside—don’t wander aimlessly from the start. The museum’s galleries span multiple floors, but its true heart lies in just *one* place: the “Ancient China” permanent exhibition on Basement Level 1 (B1). This gallery unfolds chronologically—from prehistoric times all the way through the Ming and Qing dynasties—and houses the vast majority of China’s most celebrated national treasures. So take my word for it: Head straight to B1 first—and devote your *most* energy and time there. Save upper floors for later, depending on stamina. If you reverse the order and start upstairs, by the time you descend to B1 your legs will be jelly—and you’ll end up skimming its crown jewels. That would truly be a waste.

## Which route to take? Follow mine: Go forward—no backtracking  

Once inside, don’t hesitate—go straight down to B1 and the “Ancient China” exhibition. Just follow the exhibition path at your own pace. It’s laid out chronologically—from antiquity to the imperial era—so moving forward naturally means no unnecessary backtracking. Here’s a pro tip: Skip the express elevator directly to B1—it frequently shuts down. Instead, take the escalator down: as you exit onto B1, the entrance to the Qin-Han section will be right on your *right*, helping you bypass the initial queue and avoid standing idle at the entrance.

A few must-stop pieces—I’ll call them out for you. The Shang-dynasty *Four-Goat Square Zun*: a pinnacle of ancient bronze craftsmanship, exquisitely sculpted. The Han-dynasty *Jade Burial Suit with Gold Threads*: royal funeral attire, with thousands of jade plaques meticulously stitched together with golden wire—the precision still baffles modern observers. *Tang Sancai* glazes: instantly evocative of Tang-era opulence and confidence. And one heavyweight—*Houmuwu Ding*, weighing over 800 kg, located on the second floor: stand before it, and you’ll *feel* what “awe-inspiring” truly means—don’t miss it on your way up. Then there’s Empress Xiaoduan’s *Nine-Dragon Nine-Phoenix Crown*: extravagantly intricate, perpetually crowded—time your visit for a lull, and a quick, close-up look is absolutely worth it.

## Lao Ai’s insider picks: What to see upstairs  

If you still have energy after B1, head upward. On the second floor, besides the *Houmuwu Ding*, you’ll find permanent exhibitions on ancient coins and bronze mirrors. A note: The “Road to Rejuvenation” exhibition sometimes undergoes renovation—check the official website or app *before* you go, so you don’t arrive to find it closed. The third floor regularly hosts rotating special exhibitions—past highlights include the Li Jingxun Tomb artifacts and the Sanxingdui relics. Don’t skip these temporary shows—they often deliver more surprises than the permanent collections. The fourth floor features thematic galleries on traditional costumes, porcelain, jade, and Buddhist sculptures—ideal for unhurried, deep-dive exploration. If time allows, you could easily spend half a day here.

## How to book—and how to dodge crowds  

The NMC is free, but reservations are required. Simply search “National Museum of China” in the WeChat mini-program to book your slot. For the smoothest experience, reserve an entry time between 9:00–11:00 AM—this avoids the peak arrival of tour groups, keeping crowds relatively light. If you’re carrying luggage, don’t struggle—lockers are available onsite. Storing bags lets you explore freely and comfortably.

On guided tours: Let me be honest—the NMC’s official WeChat mini-program offers excellent *free audio guides*. They’re more comprehensive—and far more comfortable—than renting the museum’s handheld audio devices (which offer incomplete content and awkward earpieces—I tried once and never again). If you happen upon a volunteer-led public tour, consider yourself lucky: listening in beats solo browsing any day.

## Lao Ai’s real-talk timing guide  

These tips come from my own missteps—or those I’ve watched others make. Short on time? Stick strictly to B1’s “Ancient China” for two hours—hit the major highlights and leave satisfied. Most visitors find 3–4 hours ideal: finish B1, then choose one or two thematic galleries or special exhibitions upstairs for deeper exploration. Got a full day? Then savor each floor’s highlights at your own pace. There’s even a café inside where you can rest—don’t push yourself.  

And yes—I *must* repeat it: wear comfortable shoes. The exhibition space is staggeringly vast; after a full day, your feet won’t feel like yours anymore. When tired, sit down—don’t force yourself to see *everything*. You simply *can’t* absorb the NMC in one visit. Leave room for wonder—and return next time with fresh eyes.
