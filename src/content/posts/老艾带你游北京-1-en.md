*A Chinese Traveler Who’s Been Everywhere in China Teaches You How to Truly Experience It*

> Hi, I’m Lao Ai. The number of times I’ve visited Beijing might sound unbelievable—even the Forbidden City alone, I’ve walked through with friends seven or eight times, and each time, I still find something new. Autumn is the season I most want to bring you here: the sky is high and clouds are sparse; when the ginkgo trees turn golden, their brilliance against the vermilion walls and gilded roofs makes every casual phone snap look stunning. But don’t believe those online posts promising “3-day checklists for all top attractions”—that’s rushing, not traveling. Follow my lead: six days, slow and unhurried—no exhaustion, just deep, authentic enjoyment. This isn’t a generic itinerary. It’s hard-won wisdom from my own missteps: how to grab tickets, which gate to enter, where to eat—everything laid bare.

**A few key points—get these straight before you go**

* **Best window**: Mid-October to early November (~40 days). Peak red leaves: late October. Full ginkgo yellow: first week of November  
* **Ticket booking**: Forbidden City—7 days ahead at 8 p.m.; National Museum—7 days ahead at 5 p.m.; Chairman Mao Memorial Hall—6 days ahead at 12:30 p.m. All via official WeChat accounts  
* **Three essentials to carry**: Soft-soled shoes (20,000 steps per day is normal); light jacket (10–15°C temperature swing between morning and evening); power bank + ID card  
* **Two ironclad rules**: Most museums close on Mondays—plan around it; use annual park passes or student IDs wherever possible—don’t pay full price needlessly  

## Day 1: The Forbidden City’s Central Axis—Master the Main Story First  

I always schedule the Forbidden City for Day 1—not for any grand reason, but because that fresh arrival buzz deserves to land squarely in this city’s most treasured place. Tian’anmen Square is free but requires advance reservation. Climbing the Tian’anmen Rostrum costs ¥15—and also needs booking 7 days ahead at 5 p.m. The Chairman Mao Memorial Hall opens only in the morning, with reservations opening 6 days ahead at 12:30 p.m. All three can easily fit into one morning. Note: There are no restrooms inside the Square, and don’t carry too much water—buy it *after* security. Use the underground passage to reach Wu Men (Meridian Gate). Don’t count on buying tickets on-site—that’s gambling, and losing is the default. If you miss out, your best souvenir will be a photo of the red wall from outside.

The real way to do it: At 8 p.m., exactly 7 days before your visit, log onto the official “Palace Museum” WeChat account. Miss this step, and everything else falls apart. Once inside Wu Men, resist the urge to sprint with your camera. Follow this smooth route: Wu Men → Taihe Dian (Hall of Supreme Harmony) → Zhonghe Dian (Hall of Central Harmony) → Baohe Dian (Hall of Preserving Harmony) → Treasure Gallery (northeast corner—easy detour) → Eastern & Western Six Palaces → Shenwu Men (Gate of Divine Might) exit. The Treasure Gallery and Clock Gallery each cost ¥10—don’t skip them. Gold, jade, and jewels dazzle so intensely, I still linger there every time. When exiting Shenwu Men, *don’t hail a taxi*. Just cross the street—Jingshan Park is right there, a two-minute walk. Save it for tomorrow. One more tip: Water inside the Palace is expensive—fill up *before* entering. For guided tours, rent an audio guide for ¥20—it’s calm, clear, and far better than shouting tour guides who’ll leave your ears ringing.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** The Forbidden City closes on Mondays—schedule around it. To photograph empty halls, arrive right at opening (9 a.m.). By 9:30 a.m., tour groups flood in—your composition will be full of backs of heads. If traveling with elders or kids, skip the full circuit. Stick to the central axis + Treasure Gallery—the rest can wait for stronger legs.

## Day 2: Jingshan for Panoramas, Then Dive into Hutong Autumn  

Day 2 is about easing in—no heavy lifting. Start with Jingshan Park, directly across from the Forbidden City’s north gate. Tickets cost just ¥2. Climb to Wanchun Pavilion, and the entire Forbidden City unfolds beneath you—a sea of golden rooftops. That iconic “Lao Ai Perspective” panorama? That’s the view. For crowd-free shots, arrive before 7 a.m.—otherwise, selfie sticks dominate the frame. After descending, stroll along Beihai Park. In autumn, cool breezes ripple across the water; willows turn gold, lotus stems stand bare—another kind of elegant spaciousness. No ticket needed—just walk the lakeside. The real highlight comes in the afternoon: the hutongs. Skip Nanluoguxiang’s main street—it’s saturated with tourist commerce. Instead, head north to Beiluoguxiang, Wudao Ying, or Dongsi—where old Beijing still breathes.

I have a habit with friends: explore hutongs *before* eating. Gray-brick courtyards, gnarled trees, autumn underwear hung to dry—this is living authenticity, absent from main streets. Nearby lies Prince Gong’s Mansion (¥40 entry; book 10 days ahead at 8 p.m.). Follow the route: Yinan Dian → Xijin Zhai → Houzhao Lou → Fu Chi Pond. See how Heshen, the Qing dynasty’s wealthiest official, cleverly concealed his treasures. As dusk settles, find a spot by Shichahai Lake. Sunset paints the water tangerine, and Houhai’s mellow rhythm begins. Bonus fact: From Yinding Bridge, you can “Yinding Guanshan” (“Silver Ding Viewing the Mountains”)—one of the historic Eight Scenic Spots of Yanjing. On clear days, you’d once see western mountain silhouettes—but today’s skyscrapers block most of it. Still, that easy, unhurried spirit remains.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** Shops labeled “Old Beijing Snacks” inside hutongs? Half are purely for tourists. For *lu zhu* (stewed offal), go to Xiao Chang Chen in Menkuan Hutong; for *chao gan* (stewed liver), try Tianxingju or Yao Ji; for *zhajiangmian*, head to Fangzhuanchang No. 69. These are locals’ trusted spots—skip the main-street impulse stops. Walk just 50 meters further, and flavor—and value—returns.

## Day 3: Temple of Heaven’s Ritual Grandeur, Qianmen & Niujie’s Vibrant Flavors  

Day 3 belongs to the Temple of Heaven. First, the biggest pitfall: tickets and combo tickets aren’t the same. A ¥15 basic ticket won’t get you into the Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests or the Echo Wall—guaranteed disappointment. Buy the ¥34 combo ticket instead. Enter via the East Gate: Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests → Danbi Bridge → Huangqiongyu (the Echo Wall sits in this small courtyard—don’t miss it) → Circular Mound Altar, then exit south. The Temple’s magic isn’t in its size—it’s in its *circles*: ancient cosmology (“Heaven is round, Earth is square”; “Harmony between Heaven and Man”) embodied in architecture. Stand at the center of the Circular Mound and shout—you’ll hear your voice echo back, wrapping around you. Small sites stop selling tickets at 5:30 p.m., and it’s closed Mondays—go early, not late.

In the afternoon, head to Qianmen Dashilar—home to clusters of time-honored brands, all walkable. For dinner, I strongly recommend Niujie (Ox Street)—the heart of Beijing’s halal cuisine. Try Hongji’s beef buns and sesame cakes, Baiji’s glutinous rice cakes, Jubao Yuan’s hotpot, or Nailao Wei’s milk rolls—affordable, authentic, and far more genuine than Qianmen’s polished restaurants. A friend once insisted on dining at the Niujie entrance—¥48 for a bowl of zhajiangmian, worse than the ¥15 version from the alleyway stall. Tell me, is that fair? After dinner, stroll back to your hotel. Today’s step count is gentler—perfect recovery before tomorrow’s garden climb.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** Niujie gets crowded at mealtime—arrive before 5:30 p.m. to shorten lines. For *yang xie zi* (spicy lamb spine), avoid random shops near tourist zones. Go to Jubao Yuan or Mabeier—locals’ favorites. Exiting the Temple of Heaven’s West Gate puts you near the Natural History Museum—ideal for families to combine both in one day.

## Day 4: The Imperial Gardens—Summer Palace & Old Summer Palace in Autumn  

Day 4 is dedicated to imperial gardens—two adjacent, perfectly sequential sites. Here’s my no-detour route for the Summer Palace: Enter via the New Palace Gate, take a boat to the Marble Boat, then walk eastward along the Long Corridor toward the East Palace Gate—covering Painting in the Clouds Pavilion, the Tower of Buddhist Incense, and more. But I’ll share a personal secret: If time allows, slip into the Back Creek River area via the North Ruyi Gate. That narrow waterside path, flanked by golden ginkgo reflections and quiet paths, is breathtaking—and known mostly to locals. Suzhou Street charges extra and lacks autumn charm—skip it. The park is vast: save your legs with boats or electric carts. And yes—water inside is pricey. Bring your own.

From there, take a few bus stops to the Old Summer Palace (Yuanmingyuan). Tickets: ¥10 (basic) or ¥25 (combo). Enter via the East Gate—the closest to the Western-style ruins: the Great Fountain Ruins, the Maze Garden, and the panoramic sand model. With autumn grass turning gold, the ruins feel especially poignant. Every time I stand before the Great Fountain, I pause—those five textbook words, “The Burning of the Old Summer Palace,” hit differently when you’re standing face-to-face with history. There’s also a 1-kilometer ginkgo avenue in the Qichun Garden section (northwest), separate from the ruins. Visit in late September for floral splendor—but don’t expect full golden glory yet. If short on time, head straight to the Western Ruins; save ginkgos for Day 6 at the Temple of Heaven.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** Both the Summer Palace and Old Summer Palace are huge—each demands half a day for proper appreciation. Don’t cram them with other sights. I dedicate Day 4 solely to these two, then rest early—so I’m ready to conquer the Great Wall on Day 5.

## Day 5: The Great Wall—Then Bird’s Nest & Water Cube by Night  

Day 5 means heading out of town—the Great Wall is non-negotiable. Without it, you haven’t truly been to Beijing. Badaling is famous and well-equipped: Take the cable car up to the North 7th Tower, then hike to the North 8th Tower (the “Hero’s Slope”)—effortless and scenic. But honestly? I prefer Mutianyu—fewer crowds, richer autumn colors, and photogenic vistas. Its cable car + toboggan ride delights kids too—though its lower profile means you’ll need to research it yourself. Avoid “one-day tours” with signs at Qianmen—they often divert you to the less impressive Juyongguan or wild sections, plus mandatory shopping stops. Instead, take the S2 suburban railway (departing from Beijing North Station, just a few yuan) or Bus 877 from Deshengmen—cheap and reliable.

The Wall is windy—tops are several degrees colder than downtown. Bring your jacket. With elders or kids, don’t force a full climb. Cable car up and down to the Hero’s Slope is enough for a meaningful stamp. If your legs ache, keep tonight light—no packed schedules. Return to the city, but don’t crash immediately. Head to the Bird’s Nest and Water Cube for night views—their soft blue glow transforms them completely, offering a stark, modern contrast to daytime’s ancient architecture. This is Beijing’s contemporary face, lit up in full. My plan: Exhausted from the Wall? Enjoy a relaxed night view and a good meal—no forced walking. Otherwise, Day 6 will feel impossible.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** The Great Wall is 1.5–2 hours from downtown. Leave before 8 a.m. for the smoothest trip—don’t pair it with distant red-leaf routes on the same day, or you’ll waste hours en route. During evening rush hour (5:30–7 p.m.), avoid surface transport—subway transfers beat taxis.

## Day 6: National Museum Finale, Ginkgo Avenue Grand Finale  

Save Day 6 for the National Museum—free but fiercely competitive. Book at 5 p.m., exactly 7 days ahead. This is my chosen finale: The Forbidden City shows *how emperors lived*; the NMC reveals *where the Chinese people came from*. Enter via the North Gate for speed, then head straight to the B1 Ancient China Exhibition—see the Houmuwu Ding bronze vessel, the Four Rams Ritual Vessel, terracotta figurines—and traverse 5,000 years in one sweep. I always spend half a day here. Carry your ID—no lighters allowed at security. Families with kids? Swap the NMC for the Natural History Museum (book 3 days ahead at 11 a.m.). Its B1 Dinosaur Park keeps children engaged for hours—and it’s near the Temple of Heaven, making it easy to combine.

Don’t waste the afternoon—head to ginkgo viewing. The most famous ginkgo avenue runs outside the Diaoyutai State Guesthouse—but it’s packed. For serene photos, go to Ditan Park (enter via West Gate—golden ginkgos against red walls; *my personal pick for Beijing’s quintessential autumn portrait*) or Zhongshan Park, or the lesser-known Sanli River (near Qianmen, riverside, fewer people). Don’t delay ginkgo shoots until mid-November—wind strips leaves fast. The first week of November is peak window. Miss it, and you wait another year. Snap your final shot—and your six-day journey concludes. Catch your flight, or extend your stay—your call.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** The National Museum closes Mondays—never schedule it then. Galleries are large and air-conditioned—bring a light long-sleeve. Food inside is overpriced and underwhelming—eat outside. You can also squeeze in Yonghegong Temple: its autumn ginkgos carpet the pathways—¥25 entry. Walk from Zhaotai Gate to Wofu Pavilion for solemnity warmed by golden light.

## Pre-Trip Essentials—Get These Sorted First  

Now that we’ve covered six days, let’s consolidate cross-site practicalities—lessons learned from my own blunders (and friends’).  

1. **Booking is life-or-death**: Forbidden City, National Museum, Chairman Mao Memorial Hall, and Natural History Museum—all booked via their official WeChat accounts. Set alarms. Don’t wait until you’re at the gate.  
2. **Transportation**: The subway is your best friend. Download the “Beijing Rail Transit” app or use Alipay’s transit QR code—no physical card needed. For buses, scan *both* when boarding *and* alighting—or you’ll be overcharged. Avoid surface transport during rush hours (7:30–9 a.m., 5:30–7 p.m.)—Lines 1, 2, and 4 become human sardine cans.  
3. **Payments**: Alipay and WeChat now support foreign cards—most places accept QR codes. But carry ¥100–200 cash for street vendors and public toilets.  
4. **Clothing**: Autumn brings big swings—20°C+ in daylight, dropping below 10°C after sunset. Layer like an onion. Soft-soled shoes matter more than anything.  
5. **Restrooms**: Lines form fast at major sites—use facilities *before* entering. Public toilets in hutongs are often cleaner than those inside parks.  
6. **Pitfalls to avoid**: Locals skip Wangfujing’s snack street; haggle aggressively at Xiu Shui Street; many “time-honored” Qianmen brands are fakes—stick to the eateries I listed earlier.  
7. **Saving money**: Student IDs = half-price; Beijing Park Annual Pass pays off if staying long-term. Many museums close Mondays—Tuesdays are quieter.  
8. **Essential apps**: Gaode Maps (navigation), Dianping (food discovery + authentic reviews), Hanglv Zongheng (flights), 12306 (trains).  
9. **Xiangshan’s red leaves?** Famous—but crowds are brutal. Weekday afternoons mean seeing heads, not leaves. Skip unless you’re obsessed. Ditan, Zhongshan, and Dajuesi or Pofengling offer equally stunning ginkgos and red leaves—with breathing room.  
10. **Shorter days**: Autumn daylight fades by 5 p.m. Don’t overload your schedule—leave evenings open for unhurried hutong strolls at golden hour.  

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip:** Beijing’s golden autumn lasts just ~40 days. Peak red leaves: late October. Full ginkgo yellow: first week of November. Hit this window, and what you see matches the photos online. Come too early—everything’s green. Too late—bare branches. Either way, you’ve missed it. This magic happens just once a year—don’t get the timing wrong.

## Where to Stay & Where to Eat  

I’ve thought this through for you. First-timers prioritizing convenience should stay near Qianmen Street, Chongwenmen, Zhushikou, Ping’anli, or Caishikou—subway hubs with dual-line access, within walking distance of Tian’anmen and the Forbidden City. Early-morning flag-raising? No alarm needed. For authentic hutong atmosphere, choose courtyard hotels around Shichahai—but *always ask in advance*: “Can cars drop off at the door?” Many inner-ring hutongs forbid vehicles—dragging luggage over stone slabs is torture, especially with elders or kids. Wangfujing and Guomao offer upscale hotels for higher budgets. Beijing South Railway Station suits high-speed rail travelers—close to the Temple of Heaven and Taoranting Park.

For food, follow this map:  
- **Snack districts**: Qianmen Dashilar, Niujie, Guijie (Ghost Street), and Huguo Temple  
- **Hotpot**: Jubao Yuan, Beimen Shuanrou, Jingmen Lao Baosan  
- **Authentic Beijing cuisine**: Shijiu, Tongchunyuan, Sishi Tongtang  
- **Hutong-style dining**: Qixun, Bajiao  
- **Iconic snacks**: Hongji, Baiji, Nailao Wei, Sanyuan Mei Yuan, Hongxing Qianjin Milk & Bakery  
- **Late-night seafood**: Hu Da (for spicy crayfish)  

One rule: Never sit down at the first restaurant you see near a tourist site. Walk 50 meters—authenticity and value return instantly. My habit? Arrive somewhere, open Dianping, search “local-frequented,” sort by *distance*—not “recommended” (which is often paid ads).

> Done. That’s your six-day Beijing. Beijing isn’t about “checking off” sights—it’s about *wandering*. Slow down, and the red walls will whisper their stories to you. When you finally come, follow this path—and you’ll enjoy it far more than any rushed checklist. Next time you want to explore another city, just tell me. I’ll take you there—from one plane ticket—to discover a China that’s vast, yet deeply gentle.
