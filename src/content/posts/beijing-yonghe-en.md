During the years I worked in Beijing, I visited countless “must-check-in” spots. Yet the place I truly want to return to isn’t the Forbidden City or the Great Wall—it’s Yonghe Temple: a Tibetan Buddhist monastery nestled right inside Beijing’s 2nd Ring Road.

## What It Is  

Originally the princely residence of Emperor Yongzheng before his ascension, Yonghe Temple was later converted into a Tibetan Buddhist monastery—and today stands as one of Beijing’s most important centers of Tibetan Buddhism. Its vermilion walls and gilded roofs glow softly; sandalwood incense lingers in the air; and monks still chant sutras daily within its courtyards. It is also a National Key Cultural Relics Protection Unit—a living cultural heritage site, not just an antique locked away in a glass case.

## My Take  

It may sound a bit mystical, but Beijingers widely say Yonghe Temple is “very efficacious.” I’m no devout believer—but whenever I feel anxious or uncertain about a decision, I always find myself drawn here for a quiet walk. Strolling beneath those red walls, breathing in the sandalwood scent, watching people kneel in prayer or whisper wishes—my heart inexplicably calms. Amid this city’s relentless bustle, Yonghe Temple remains a rare sanctuary where you can simply catch your breath.

## Why Foreigners Are Captivated  

For foreigners, Yonghe Temple offers a doorway into understanding “Chinese spiritual life”—not through religious doctrine, but through lived, everyday faith. Spinning prayer wheels, prostrations, offering butter lamps—all resonate with Tibetan Buddhist practices they’ve seen in Tibet or Bhutan, yet here they unfold at the very heart of a modern metropolis. This “sacred space amid urban clamor” moves people deeply—and most effectively shatters the stereotype that China offers nothing but tourist attractions.

## Where to Go · How to Begin  

Exit directly from Yonghe Temple Station (Line 2/5). Visit early in the morning when crowds are light. Walk clockwise through the temple grounds, keep your voice low, and respectfully observe those engaged in worship. Incense can be purchased at the entrance. Afterward, stroll down Wudao Ying Hutong, grab a cup of coffee, and linger a little longer with that quiet calm.
