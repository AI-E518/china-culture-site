A German friend finished an entire table of dishes and politely remarked: “Shanghai cuisine is sweet!” I glanced at the menu and laughed—Sweet-and-Sour Spare Ribs, Eight-Treasure Duck, and Osmanthus-Scented Lotus Root. He’d ordered the three *sweetest*-named dishes on the menu.

It’s not his fault. On English-language menus, these three are the easiest to recognize—and their photos are the most eye-catching. But it’s like visiting Paris and ordering only three crème brûlées, then concluding, “French food is sweet.”

I’ve lived in Shanghai for over twenty years, and the single most misunderstood element of *Benbang* (Shanghai-style) cuisine is precisely this touch of sugar.

## That Touch of Sugar? It’s *Working*, Not Serving as Dessert

Shanghai cooking emphasizes **“rich oil, deep soy sauce”** (*nóng yóu chì jiàng*): generous oil, thick soy-based sauces, and ample cooking time—resulting in glossy, deeply colored dishes. Here, sugar’s job is to thicken the sauce, deepen the color, and lift the savory-umami depth—not to deliver overt sweetness.

![](/media/uploads/上海红烧肉，甜糯入味太下饭了！🍖_1_味蕾之旅_来自小红书网页版.jpg)

Take *hongshao rou* (braised pork belly). A well-made version hits your palate in layers: first rich soy aroma, then tender fat and meat, finally a subtle, clean finish of gentle sweetness lingering at the back of the tongue—never cloying. If the first bite punches you with sugary intensity, the sugar was overused—or worse, the dish was specially adjusted for tourists.

A French analogy makes it clearer: this sugar functions more like the *glaze* in a slow-braised French stew—built through long reduction to coat the meat—rather than a dessert topping. It’s kin to Japanese *teriyaki*, but far more restrained. At any reputable *Benbang* restaurant, you’ll usually find a small dish of vinegar or shredded ginger on the table—precisely to cut through that rich oiliness.

*Benbang* cuisine wasn’t created in isolation. Its roots lie in local Shanghai home cooking, then gradually absorbed and adapted strengths from Huizhou, Huaiyang, Suzhou–Wuxi, and Cantonese cuisines—evolving into what we know today. Historic restaurants like Lao Zheng Xing (founded 1862), Rong Shun Guan (1875—the predecessor of today’s Shanghai Old Restaurant), and De Xing Guan (1883) began refining this hybrid style right after the port opened. So calling it “not Chinese enough” *or* “too Western” misses the point entirely: it was born mixed.

## My Ordering Formula for Foreign Friends

Don’t order by dish name—order by *role*. For four people, four to five dishes is perfect:

**One Cold Appetizer** — Smoked Fish, Poached Chicken, or Four-Joy Braised Wheat Gluten. Pick one. Smoked fish is fried then marinated—it’s crisp outside, tender within—and many foreigners “get it” instantly.

**One Red-Braised Dish** — *Hongshao rou* (braised pork belly), or *Caotou Quanzi* (stewed pork intestine with caotou—tender alfalfa sprouts). Anyone bold enough to order the latter earns my respect; it’s at its absolute best in spring.

**One Fresh & Vibrant Dish** — Shrimp Stir-Fried in Oil, Crab Roe Tofu, or Steamed Yellow Croaker—choose according to season. River shrimp shells become so crisp-fried you can eat them whole.

**One Soup** — *Yan Du Xian* (cured ham, fresh bamboo shoots, and pork soup). But timing matters: it relies on seasonal bamboo shoots—either winter or spring shoots. It won’t taste right if it’s not cold enough; don’t force it in November—the result will likely disappoint.

**One Staple** — Scallion Oil Noodles or Vegetable Rice. Don’t underestimate that bowl of noodles: mastering scallion oil is often the first test for apprentices in *Benbang* kitchens.

![](/media/uploads/上海本帮菜的定海神针_1_南蛮虾_来自小红书网页版.jpg)

This sequence balances richness, clarity, freshness, and substance. Order this way—and you won’t get a single overly sweet bite.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: Upon entering, glance at what locals are eating. If you see *hongshao rou* served with a simple bowl of plain rice, chances are high the braising sauce is authentic. If every table features meticulously plated dishes with decorative sauce “halos,” that kitchen is cooking for cameras—not for flavor.

## In November: One Thing You *Must* Try—and One You *Shouldn’t*

I’m writing this in November—the month Shanghai waits for **crab**.

At this time, hairy crabs are plump and rich. Their roe enriches tofu, noodles, and pan-fried buns. Wangjia Sha’s crab roe xiao long bao and Guangming Cun’s version are strictly seasonal—available only from September to November. They’re taken off the menu once the season ends; no frozen substitutes allowed. You’ll rarely find this tip in English travel guides—because it’s not year-round. And *that’s exactly why it’s worth mentioning.*

Conversely, skip **Yan Du Xian** this month. It depends on bamboo shoots—winter or spring varieties—neither of which peaks in November. What you’ll get is likely a substitute, lacking true depth and fragrance. Same goes for *caotou*: tenderest in spring, tough and fibrous by autumn.

Shanghainese eat by solar terms—not by menus. Menus exist for infrequent visitors.

A simple, useful phrase: **“Jīn zhāo yǒu shá shí lìng cài?”** (“What’s in season today?”). Ask it, close the menu, and let the staff decide for you—it’s far more reliable than scrolling through glossy photo menus.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: Don’t ask while pointing at the English menu. Ask the server or manager directly. Many seasonal dishes never appear on printed menus—they’re written on a small chalkboard by the door or shared only among regulars. Some of the best dishes I’ve ordered for friends came from asking exactly this question.

## Places I Generally *Don’t* Recommend

I tend to avoid those bright, glossy restaurants along Nanjing East Road with English signage declaring “Authentic Shanghai Cuisine.” Not every one is bad—but the rent and turnover rate at that location mean kitchens simply can’t take the time to slowly reduce a proper sauce.

Same goes for the overpriced crab roe xiao long bao near the Nine-Twist Bridge in Yuyuan Garden. You’re queuing for location—not flavor.

Another red flag: the more photos on the menu, and the longer the bilingual descriptive blurbs per dish, the more cautious you should be. Traditional *Benbang* establishments often have menus so plain they border on austere—because they rely on repeat customers, not your one-time visit.

A modest, unassuming spot charging ¥80–¥150 per person is usually safer than a sleekly designed one. I don’t blindly trust rankings—but I *do* trust one thing: at noon, if there are more diners speaking Shanghainese than English inside, you’re probably in good hands.

> **Lao Ai’s Insider Tip**: If you avoid offal, dislike scallions, or have allergies to peanuts or shrimp, state it clearly *before* ordering. *Benbang* kitchens use dried shrimp, lard, and Shaoxing wine far more than English menus reveal—and none of these appear in translated descriptions. Jot your dietary restrictions in your phone’s notes app and show the Chinese text to staff. It’s faster—and more accurate—than gesturing.

## One Final, Honest Note

I won’t urge you to “fall in love with *Benbang* cuisine” at first bite. The saltiness of *Yan Du Xian*, the intensity of *Caotou Quanzi*, the fermented tang of *zao* (marinated) dishes—these can all feel unfamiliar at first. Among friends I’ve introduced to Shanghai food, some frowned on the spot—only to return the next day for a bowl of scallion oil noodles.

Eating isn’t about instant verdicts. Order right—and you’ll earn yourself a second chance.
