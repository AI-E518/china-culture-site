## First, Understand Its Layout

Don’t rush in snapping photos the second you cross the threshold. Plain and simple—the Forbidden City isn’t *a building*. It’s *a city*—and like any well-planned city, it has a clear “skeleton.” Broadly speaking, it’s divided into two parts: a central north–south axis—the undisputed star of the show—that runs straight from the southern gate to the northern gate; and two symmetrical wings flanking it—the Eastern and Western Routes—where imperial consorts once lived. If it’s your first visit, take my word for it: Stick closely to the central axis. The most precious, most historically significant spaces are all aligned along it. Everything else? Save it for next time. Enter through the Meridian Gate, then follow the path northward past the Hall of Supreme Harmony, the Palace of Heavenly Purity, and beyond—you’ll experience every key ceremonial space in proper sequence. That’s how most visitors do it—and for good reason.

## Along the Central Axis: The Outer Court and Inner Court

Even the central axis holds a subtle division. Right at its heart lies the Gate of Heavenly Purity—*Qianqing Men*. South of the gate is the **Outer Court**, where the emperor conducted state affairs and held grand ceremonies. North of the gate lies the **Inner Court**, where the emperor, empress, and consorts actually lived. Here’s the simplest way to remember it: *“Work up front, live in back.”* The Outer Court’s showstoppers are the Three Great Halls—the Hall of Supreme Harmony, the Hall of Central Harmony, and the Hall of Preserving Harmony—the most majestic structures in the entire complex, embodying imperial authority itself. The Inner Court centers on the “Three Rear Palaces”: the Palace of Heavenly Purity, the Hall of Union, and the Palace of Earthly Tranquility—surrounded by the famed Six Eastern Palaces and Six Western Palaces. In short: *outer court for governance, inner court for residence.* This fundamental principle has governed Chinese imperial palace design for millennia—and remembering it unlocks the logic behind everything you’ll see.

## The Eastern and Western Routes: Consorts’ Courtyards

Now, a quick word about the side courtyards. The Eastern and Western Routes were primarily residential quarters for imperial consorts and dowager empresses—and even retired emperors, like Qianlong, spent their later years here. Compared with the solemn grandeur of the central axis, these courtyards feel smaller, cozier, more human—closer to everyday life. If you want to imagine *how people actually lived inside the palace*, this is exactly where you should wander.

## Lao Ai’s Insider Take: Why the Central Axis Is Non-Negotiable

I’ve brought countless friends here—and I can never resist adding this one point. Our ancestors believed firmly in *“central position equals supreme dignity.”* Every most prestigious, most symbolically charged space in the Forbidden City sits squarely on that central axis—symmetrical, layered, and meticulously ordered inward. Once you grasp the rhythm and meaning of that axis, you’ve truly *read* the Forbidden City—it literally inscribes the phrase *“imperial supremacy”* into every brick and beam. So when I say “stick to the central axis on your first visit,” it’s not about taking the easy way out. It’s about focusing on what matters most.

## Don’t Miss: The Treasure Gallery and the Furniture Gallery

Just admiring buildings isn’t enough. The Palace Museum also hosts several thematic galleries—and I highly recommend the **Treasure Gallery** and the **Furniture Gallery**. The Treasure Gallery dazzles with imperial gold, jade, gems, and ornaments—so radiant they’ll make your eyes water. The Furniture Gallery reveals how palace life was arranged and lived—down to the smallest details of daily comfort. Heads-up: Both require separate admission tickets—don’t wait until you reach the entrance to find out!

## Visitor Tips

Let me give you the essentials: The Palace Museum is closed every **Monday**. Always enter via the **Meridian Gate**, and exit via the **Shenwu Gate**. Plan for *at least half a day*—walking here is surprisingly strenuous, so wear comfortable shoes. And if your feet start to ache? Don’t push yourself. Slow down. Stroll. That’s *real* Forbidden City visiting.

## Fun Fact: Why Are There So Many Crows in the Forbidden City?

As you wander, you’ll surely notice them—crows wheeling overhead in large, dark flocks. Many visitors quietly wonder: *“Is this place… spiritually heavy?”* Let me reassure you—this has absolutely nothing to do with superstition. The crow population is the result of centuries-old tradition *plus* today’s favorable ecological conditions—two forces converging over time.

First, the tradition. During the Qing Dynasty, the Manchu people revered crows as “divine birds.” Legend tells of Nurhaci—founder of the Qing—being pursued by enemies. In desperation, he feigned death and lay motionless on the ground—until crows suddenly descended and settled upon him. From afar, his pursuers mistook the scene for an ordinary corpse and passed by. To repay this life-saving intervention, the Qing imperial family erected a special “Solen Pole” inside the Forbidden City—a tall wooden pillar used for sky worship, topped with offerings of pork offal and broken rice—exclusively for feeding crows. This official feeding program lasted for hundreds of years, and crows gradually established their home here.

Then, modern ecology took over. Though the Qing Dynasty ended long ago, crows stayed—because the Forbidden City remains an ideal habitat.\
**First, safety:** Its towering palaces and dense, interlocking roof brackets offer countless hidden, dry nooks perfect for nesting. At night, after closing time, the compound becomes quiet and secluded—and those high vermilion walls keep out natural predators like stray cats and weasels.\
**Second, warmth:** Beijing’s urban core is significantly warmer than its suburbs due to the “urban heat island” effect—often several degrees higher. For crows, the Forbidden City—right in the city center—is a winter refuge. They fly to suburban areas by day to forage, then return en masse at dusk to roost.\
**Third, food security:** Ancient trees abound—pine, cypress, ginkgo—their seeds and fruits provide steady nourishment. And surrounding neighborhoods offer abundant food sources too.

So next time you see a flock of crows soaring overhead, don’t spook yourself. They chose the Forbidden City simply because it’s comfortable, safe—and the optimal living solution forged by centuries of history *and* this unique ecosystem. Today, these crows have become a living part of the Palace Museum’s landscape—guarding history while coexisting peacefully with nature.
