*A Chinese Traveler Who’s Been Everywhere in China Teaches You How to Experience It Right*

> Hi, I’m Lao Ai. I’ve been to Beijing more than ten times—and climbed several sections of the Great Wall: Badaling, Mutianyu, Simatai… Once, I even signed up for a “one-day tour” and ended up sitting idly on some unknown, unmarked hillside. So yes—you’ve asked the right person. Let me tell you how to truly experience the Great Wall, not just tick it off your bucket list.

**First, a few key facts—keep these in mind**

* **Official Name**: The Great Wall of China (what you see today is mostly the Ming Dynasty section)  
* **Age**: Built intermittently over more than 2,000 years—first connected under the Qin Dynasty, most impressively rebuilt and reinforced with brick and stone during the Ming Dynasty  
* **Length**: Over 21,000 km total; the Ming section alone stretches about 8,800 km  
* **Most Famous Sections Near Beijing**: Badaling, Mutianyu, Simatai, Jinshanling  
* **UNESCO Status**: Inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 1987  
* **Iconic Quote**: *“He who has never been to the Great Wall is not a true man.”* — engraved on the “Hero Slope” at Badaling

## First, Understand This: The Great Wall Isn’t One Single Stretch—It’s a System

Let’s clear this up right away—don’t say “I’m going to climb the Great Wall” like it’s one thing. It’s not a wall. It’s a vast, evolving frontier defense system built across dynasties—from the Warring States period onward. The Qin Dynasty linked pre-existing walls; the Ming Dynasty fortified them with brick and stone, creating the strongest, most enduring sections we see today. Almost all accessible sections near Beijing are Ming-era. So here’s my advice: don’t aim to “climb the Great Wall”—you couldn’t finish 21,000 km in a lifetime. Pick one section and explore it deeply. That’s more than enough.

## Which Section Should You Choose? Here’s My Honest Take

There are many climbable sections near Beijing—I’ll share my real-world impressions of the most popular ones.  
- **Badaling** is the most famous and best-equipped. I usually take first-time visitors here for peace of mind: cable cars and lifts make it easy for elders and kids. But during holidays? The Hero Slope turns into a “Hero Pile”—crowded beyond belief.  
- **Mutianyu** is my personal favorite. Far fewer crowds than Badaling. In autumn, the hillsides blaze with crimson *paulownia* leaves—the colors are so vivid, you’ll find yourself snapping photos nonstop. Ride the cable car up, then zoom down on the toboggan slide. Foreign travelers love it for its quiet charm.  
- **Simatai**, paired with the nearby Gubei Water Town, is the *only* section open for night visits. This stretch is steep and dramatic; below lies Gubei Water Town—a beautifully reconstructed ancient-style town. Perfect for an overnight stay: stroll under lantern light, photograph the illuminated ramparts, or simply sit and soak it all in.  
- **Jinshanling** is where serious photographers gather—dense watchtowers, bold undulations along the ridgeline, and raw, cinematic grandeur.  
- Others worth mentioning: **Juyongguan** and **Huanghuacheng Water Great Wall**, where the wall plunges directly into a lake—an unusually poetic sight.

If you’re visiting Beijing for the first time and short on time: choose **Badaling or Mutianyu**.  
If you want stunning photos *without* the crowds: head straight to **Mutianyu or Jinshanling**.  
If you’re after romance and a memorable overnight: book **Gubei Water Town + Simatai**.  
As for me? I sneak off to **Mutianyu** whenever I need quiet time—fewer people means I can linger, frame shots carefully, and never fight for a viewpoint.

## Lao Ai’s Insider Insight: What Was the Great Wall *Really* For?

Every time I guide friends up the Wall, I always pause atop a watchtower and gesture around. Remember: the Great Wall wasn’t built for tourism—it was built for war. Beacon towers sent smoke or fire signals; watchtowers housed garrisons; the ramparts blocked cavalry charges. As you walk past those staggered, uneven towers, picture soldiers hunkered down here centuries ago—scanning the northern horizon, lighting signal fires at the first sign of movement, passing warnings from tower to tower down the line. As for *Meng Jiangnu Weeping Over the Great Wall*? That’s folklore—lovely to hear, but not history. The real story is this: Qin Shi Huang connected existing walls; the Ming Dynasty rebuilt them in brick and stone. Most of what remains today is Ming. Oh—and that famous quote? It’s from Mao Zedong. Carved into the Hero Slope at Badaling, shouting it aloud at the top feels oddly powerful.

## Getting There: Transport Tips & Pitfalls to Avoid

For **Badaling**, I strongly recommend the suburban S2 railway line—departing from Beijing North Station or Qinghe Station. It costs just a few yuan, runs like a metro, and avoids traffic jams entirely. Alternatively, take Bus 877 from Deshengmen.  
For **Mutianyu**, board the dedicated shuttle bus from Dongzhimen.  
For **Simatai**, take the Gubei Water Town express bus.  
Driving? Just enter the section name into your navigation app and follow the Jingzang Expressway.

Here’s the *critical* warning—learned the hard way: Years ago, I trusted a cheap “Great Wall One-Day Tour” flyer near Qianmen. They took us to a fake wall, forced shopping stops, and tacked on hidden meal fees. Total disaster. So—don’t believe street-side signs near Qianmen or Tian’anmen Square. Use official public transport or licensed ride-hailing apps instead. It’s cheaper, safer, and far less stressful.

## Lao Ai’s Ground Truth: What to Watch Out For While Climbing

These tips come from real stumbles—mine and others’.  
- **Wear proper shoes**: The Wall is steep, uneven, and strewn with loose stones. No high heels. No flip-flops. I’ve seen people in leather shoes give up halfway.  
- **Pack a light jacket**: Mountain winds are strong, and temperatures swing sharply between day and night. Tuck a layer into your bag—even if it’s warm when you start.  
- **Bring your own food and water**: Vendors on the Wall charge exorbitant prices for subpar snacks and drinks. A bottle of water can cost ¥15+! I always carry one bottle and an energy bar. Sitting atop a watchtower, unwrapping that bar—it’s pure bliss.  
- **Book tickets ahead**: Buy online via official WeChat accounts or platforms like Ctrip. During peak season, on-site lines are long—and tickets may sell out. Admission is ~¥40 (subject to official pricing), plus separate fees for cable cars.  
- **Stay on open, maintained sections**: “Wild” sections like Jiankou are undeveloped, breathtaking—but also dangerous. Accidents *have* happened. Don’t risk it.  
- **Use the restroom *before* you climb**: Toilets at the entrance often have long queues. Don’t wait until halfway up—trust me.
